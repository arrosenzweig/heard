1. Motivation

Heard began with the author's own experience. In her words:

"I am someone who has struggled a lot with keeping to my values and morals, even if they aren't seen as important to others. Especially in the MBA there is a lot of temptation to follow the majority opinion or cross boundaries that had previously been firm for myself."

She built it "to help myself and others have a resource to turn to when life is busy, your religious community is bleak, and when you need someone to listen without judging."

Who it is for

"Anyone who is struggling with their bad decisions, or decisions they have yet to make, or decisions that they have no power in."

Why "Heard"

"At the end of the day, people need to be heard for their true strifes and get real advice from a biblical rather than worldly perspective."

The motto, "I don't understand, but I want to understand. That's why I need you God," comes from one of her favorite gospel songs, "Why God" by Austin French (from his 2018 album Wide Open).

What is missing today

From her own search for this kind of help: "There is no AI that tailors the content based on niche beliefs and values. The only apps out there offer general evangelical or highly specialized content. There isn't an adaptable app for each person's own journey and values."

She draws the line at advice, not search: "The market might have AI scripture search, but it doesn't have the tailored and customizable advice. That advice comes inlaid with occasional scripture sharing and what that piece of scripture means." Section 2 tests this against two well-known apps and a plain chatbot.

From the start she asked for Bible verses, plain-language advice, perspectives from different denominations, and a way to connect with other Christians for community or to talk when needed.

Quotations are the author's own words, lightly edited for spelling.

2. What is new

The claim here is narrow. We compared Heard with two well-known faith apps and with a plain chatbot prompt; we did not survey the market more widely.

ProductWhat it is built around (from public descriptions)
HallowCatholic prayer and meditation: guided audio prayer, daily readings, Rosary and Lectio Divina, community challenges. Subscription ($9.99/month or $69.99/year in the US).
YouVersion Bible AppFree Bible app: many translations, audio Bibles, reading plans, studying with friends. A 2026 roundup reports an AI "life-situation" scripture search; we could not confirm this from a second source.
Plain chatbot prompt (our baseline)A single prompt asking for Christian advice with verses. No stored profile of the user's tradition, no verification step, no memory, and no way to connect the user with people.

What Heard does that we did not find in the public descriptions of these two apps:

  • Faith asked once, then used everywhere. Setup starts with "for myself" or "a church". A person picks one or more of 14 traditions (including "exploring") and answers 14 contested questions one at a time, or in their own words by voice; Heard restates it and uses it only after the user approves. Counsel speaks from inside that frame.
  • Correction with love. Replies name the wrong plainly and never condemn the person, on the model of John 8:11.
  • Verified scripture from the user's own canon. Search runs over the full text of three public-domain translations (97,993 verses); every quoted verse is checked against that text before display.
  • Accountability with memory. Check-ins are a short conversation that recalls what the user actually said ("you told me about the fight with Kyle...") and proposes a log the user confirms. A three-tap version costs nothing.
  • An SOS button for the moment of temptation. Verses appear instantly, then Heard asks what is happening and answers briefly with one action for the next ten minutes. Installable, with a home-screen shortcut.
  • Awareness. A reflective profile (type, tendencies, strengths, struggles, pitfalls, practices) in which every insight must cite the user's own history and no names appear.
  • Sponsor-style fellowship and church matching. People who came through a struggle rank first; filters for gender, age, marital status, denomination and years as a Christian; adults only. Churches self-register and get a transparent match %.
  • Voice throughout. Every text box takes speech; voice mode speaks replies aloud in a chosen natural voice and speed.

5. Safety

Risks

  • Missing a crisis, or a false alarm that teaches people not to be honest.
  • Fabricated scripture, told to someone in the moment they're most inclined to believe it.
  • Misreading someone's faith, or a check-in "remembering" something the user never said.
  • A profile that leaks details, such as the names of people the user confided about.
  • Predatory contact, including adults being connected with minors.

Defenses

  • Triage on every message (Talk, SOS, check-ins, custom struggles). Crisis halts with hand-written guidance and real US hotlines; a narrow calibration gives outward-facing, non-physical cases counsel plus resources; if triage fails twice, resources show anyway.
  • Citation verifier on every quoted verse; the real text is always what's displayed.
  • Approval loop for own-words beliefs; grounding guard on check-in memory (any name not in the user's own words rejects the question).
  • Awareness guards: every card must cite real history items; cards naming people are dropped; evidence labels stripped.
  • Fellowship is adults only: no age, or under 18, means not shown, not suggested, and unable to send or receive requests. Contact details unlock only after both accept. Church accounts can't use person features.

6. Impact

Why the matching mechanic matters. Heard's fellowship design borrows from sponsorship in 12-step recovery. In a prospective study, Tonigan and Rice found that having an AA sponsor predicted greater abstinence during early affiliation, after controlling for motivation and treatment (Psychology of Addictive Behaviors, 2010, 24(3):397-403, doi:10.1037/a0019013; 182 participants with complete data). These details come from summaries of the paper; we could not access the full text. One study is not proof; we cite it as a reason the mechanic is worth testing.

7. Limits and next steps

  • Claude path unmeasured; re-run all three evaluations with an Anthropic key before quoting Claude costs.
  • Safety evaluation is small and written: 14 explicit fixtures; indirect or coded language is untested. Next: a larger set reviewed by someone with crisis-line training. Crisis resources are US-only.
  • Reminders don't reach people yet. Check-ins happen in the app; there are no notifications or texts.
  • Voice depends on the browser: listening needs Chrome or Edge (Chrome sends audio to Google); natural voices depend on the browser (Edge has the best). Server-side speech was tested on the course key but its models are not deployed.
  • The home-screen shortcut needs hosting. It installs on desktop now; phones need Heard hosted online.
  • Awareness is a reflection, not a validated instrument, and says so.
  • ESV and NASB. Copyrighted, so not bundled. ESV is available through Crossway's API (free for non-commercial use, with a key, attribution, a link to esv.org, and at most 500 verses stored); NASB appears available through API.Bible's free plan, terms to confirm.
  • Retrieval is lexical; semantic retrieval is the next upgrade. Moderation of person-to-person contact is limited to accept, decline and block. Tradition notes are the model's general framing, by design, and should be reviewed by members of each tradition.
  • Demo data: 14 demo people and 8 demo churches, all fictional and labeled; demo profiles accept connection requests automatically so the flow can be shown, and real people always decide.
